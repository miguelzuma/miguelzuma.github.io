Main programme: gravitational lensing of waves and compact objects

A gravitational wave that passes near a mass does not simply get brighter. At the wavelengths LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA and LISA observe, the wave diffracts and interferes with itself, and the resulting frequency-dependent pattern encodes the mass, the profile and even the substructure of the lens. The same is true of any phase-coherent signal, fast radio bursts included. That makes lensing of coherent sources a probe of objects that emit no light at all — and, because a lens breaks the symmetry of the background, a test of gravity itself. This is the programme funded by the GLOW ERC Consolidator Grant.

Wave optics and gravitational-wave lensing

Geometric optics — the picture of light rays bending around a mass — breaks down when the wavelength becomes comparable to the Schwarzschild radius of the lens. For gravitational waves that happens for lens masses of order 102–105 M☉ in the LIGO band, and far higher masses for LISA. In this wave-optics regime the amplification factor becomes frequency dependent, producing a characteristic modulation across the signal rather than a simple overall magnification.

That frequency dependence is what makes the effect useful. A single lensed event constrains the lens mass and the shape of its density profile, because different profiles diffract differently. It also breaks the magnification–distance degeneracy that plagues lensing in the geometric limit: a diffracted signal carries an absolute mass scale, not just a relative one.

We derived efficient methods for computing these amplification factors, validated them against symmetric lenses where analytic results exist, and extended them to general matter distributions. The resulting machinery is public as the GLoW code, and it is what allowed us to analyse GW231123 — the first compelling candidate for a magnified and diffracted black-hole merger.

Dark matter and small-scale structure

Cold dark matter and its alternatives — warm, fuzzy, self-interacting, primordial black holes — agree on large scales and disagree below the galactic one. That is precisely where the observational evidence is thinnest, because sub-galactic halos contain few or no stars. Lensing does not care: it responds to mass, not to light.

Two complementary handles come out of this. Individual diffracted events constrain individual lenses, their masses and their profiles. And even when no single lens dominates, the accumulated effect of many small structures along the line of sight leaves a coherent stochastic imprint on the signal — a "lens stochastic diffraction" that behaves like a noise term with a very specific spectral shape. For LISA, that imprint is sensitive to halos in the 10–104 M☉ range that no other method reaches.

The electromagnetic side of the same argument gave one of the sharpest existing limits on compact dark matter: if a large fraction of dark matter were in stellar-mass black holes, distant type Ia supernovae would be measurably magnified. They are not, which rules out LIGO-mass black holes as the bulk of dark matter.

Black holes as gravitational lenses

Black holes are usually studied as sources. They are also excellent lenses, and a binary black hole is a lens whose caustic structure moves. When a sub-parsec supermassive binary sits in front of stars in its host galaxy, those caustics sweep across background stars repeatedly, once per orbit, producing a quasi-periodic magnification with a very distinctive shape.

This turns an existing problem on its head. Sub-parsec supermassive binaries are the systems that will dominate the LISA and pulsar-timing signal, and they are almost impossible to resolve directly. Quasi-periodic lensed starlight offers a way to find them electromagnetically, measure their orbital parameters, and so predict their gravitational-wave emission before it is observed.

The same physics applies closer to home. Continuous gravitational waves from sources behind Sgr A* can be strongly lensed well beyond the Einstein radius, and identifying such signals would give a direct handle on the mass distribution in the Galactic Centre.

Strong-field and multi-messenger lensing

Close to a massive body, the standard lensing formalism — weak fields, small deflections, a single flat propagation background — no longer applies. The wave couples to the background curvature at subleading order in the wavelength, and its two polarizations propagate differently. This gravitational spin-Hall effect makes the trajectory polarization dependent, an effect with no counterpart in the geometric-optics picture.

Multi-messenger lensing is the other frontier. A lensed gravitational wave and a lensed electromagnetic counterpart of the same event constrain the lens far better than either alone, because they probe complementary regimes: the wave diffracts, the light does not. Building this into a coherent observational strategy across LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA, Einstein Telescope, Rubin and Euclid is a community-scale effort, and I coordinated the microlensing section of the community white paper on it.

Fast radio bursts and coherent radio sources

Gravitational waves are not the only phase-coherent signals reaching us from cosmological distances. Fast radio bursts — brief, powerful extragalactic transients — are coherent too, and the next generation of radio observatories will detect them in numbers large enough for lensing to become a statistical tool. Because radio wavelengths are far longer than optical ones, wave-optics effects appear for much smaller lenses: FRBs can reach down to planet-scale masses that no other probe touches.

Radio sources also carry an effect gravitational waves do not. Free electrons along the line of sight produce plasma lensing, dispersive (scaling as 1/f2) and opposite in sign to gravitational deflection — light scattered into a rainbow. Plasma lensing already matters for pulsar timing and for probing the interstellar and circumgalactic medium, but it has largely been treated in the geometric limit. Developing wave-optics plasma lensing is one of the programme's theory goals, and it feeds back into the interpretation of FRB emission itself.

Further ahead, the same machinery applies wherever a coherent source meets a small lens: plasma diffraction in pulsars as a probe of the interstellar medium, and diffraction of optical and X-ray sources in searches for asteroid-mass primordial black holes.

Tests of gravity and dark energy with lensed waves

A lens does something a smooth cosmological background cannot: it breaks the symmetry. In theories beyond general relativity that lets a gravitational wave interact with new fields of different spin — couplings that vanish exactly on a homogeneous background. The result is a family of observable effects with no counterpart in Einstein's theory: birefringence, where the two polarizations travel at different speeds so the lens acts like an anisotropic crystal; dispersion, as in plasma lensing; and modified diffraction.

This makes lensing a test of gravity that needs no electromagnetic counterpart. We searched for lens-induced birefringence across 43 events in the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA catalogue and obtained bounds comparable to those from the GW170817 neutron-star merger — from gravitational-wave data alone. The constraints improve sharply once multiply-imaged events are found.

The programme extends this in two directions: from the high-frequency expansion to a complete wave-optics formalism, and from specific dark-energy models to a theory-agnostic parameterization of lensing beyond Einstein — the analogue of the post-Newtonian parameters in the Solar System. The end product is a likelihood module usable in cosmological analyses alongside large-scale structure surveys.

Methods, inference and public software

Wave-optics lensing is computationally awkward: the amplification factor is an oscillatory integral over a Fermat potential with singular structure, and a naive evaluation is far too slow to sit inside a parameter-estimation loop. A large part of the programme is therefore methodological — finding formulations that are both accurate and fast enough to run on catalogues rather than on single events.

The results are public. GLoW implements contour-integration and time-domain methods for general matter distributions and has been used in more than eighteen publications. Alongside it, collaborators lead Glworia for Bayesian parameter estimation and WOlensing for non-symmetric lenses. Everything we publish should be reproducible with code someone else can download.

The same commitment predates the lensing work: hi_class, our Einstein–Boltzmann solver for general scalar–tensor theories, has been used in more than eighty publications by groups other than ours.

Other active research

Gravitational-wave propagation and the speed of gravity

Before lensing offered a sharper handle, the cleanest test of cosmological gravity was the propagation of gravitational waves across a smooth Universe. My earlier work identified the speed of gravitational waves as the sharpest discriminant among scalar–tensor theories — a prediction settled spectacularly by GW170817, whose near-simultaneous electromagnetic counterpart ruled out entire classes of dark-energy models in a single stroke.

That line continues, and it is where the lensing-based tests in the main programme grew from.

Disformal transformations

Field redefinitions that mix the metric with derivatives of a scalar field generalize the notion of Einstein and Jordan frames. They connect disformally coupled theories to DBI Galileons, provide a screening mechanism for fifth forces, and supply much of the technical apparatus behind the beyond-Horndeski results.

Inhomogeneous cosmologies and data assumptions

Non-standard cosmologies are useful even when they are wrong: they expose the model-dependent assumptions buried in standard analysis pipelines. Our work on Lemaître–Tolman–Bondi void models showed how cosmic rulers strain inhomogeneous alternatives to dark energy.
