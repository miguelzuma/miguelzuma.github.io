Main programme: gravitational lensing of waves and compact objects

A gravitational wave that passes near a mass does not simply get brighter. At the wavelengths LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA and LISA observe, the wave diffracts and interferes with itself, and the resulting frequency-dependent pattern encodes the mass, the profile and even the substructure of the lens. The same is true of any phase-coherent signal. That makes lensing a probe of objects that emit no light at all — and, because a lens breaks the symmetry of the background, a test of gravity itself. This is the programme funded by the GLOW ERC Consolidator Grant.

Wave optics and gravitational-wave lensing

Geometric optics — the picture of light rays bending around a mass — breaks down when the wavelength becomes comparable to the Schwarzschild radius of the lens. For gravitational waves that happens for lens masses of order 102–105 M☉ in the LIGO band, and far higher masses for LISA. In this wave-optics regime the amplification factor becomes frequency dependent, producing a characteristic modulation across the signal rather than a simple overall magnification.

That frequency dependence is what makes the effect useful. A single lensed event constrains the lens mass and the shape of its density profile, because different profiles diffract differently. It also breaks the magnification–distance degeneracy that plagues lensing in the geometric limit: a diffracted signal carries an absolute mass scale, not just a relative one.

We derived efficient methods for computing these amplification factors, validated them against symmetric lenses where analytic results exist, and extended them to general matter distributions. The resulting machinery is public as the GLoW code, and it is what allowed us to analyse GW231123 — the first compelling candidate for a magnified and diffracted black-hole merger.

Dark matter and small-scale structure

Cold dark matter and its alternatives — warm, fuzzy, self-interacting, primordial black holes — agree on large scales and disagree below the galactic one. That is precisely where the observational evidence is thinnest, because sub-galactic halos contain few or no stars. Lensing does not care: it responds to mass, not to light.

Two complementary handles come out of this. Individual diffracted events constrain individual lenses, their masses and their profiles. And even when no single lens dominates, the accumulated effect of many small structures along the line of sight leaves a coherent stochastic imprint on the signal — a "lens stochastic diffraction" that behaves like a noise term with a very specific spectral shape. For LISA, that imprint is sensitive to halos in the 10–104 M☉ range that no other method reaches.

The electromagnetic side of the same argument gave one of the sharpest existing limits on compact dark matter: if a large fraction of dark matter were in stellar-mass black holes, distant type Ia supernovae would be measurably magnified. They are not, which rules out LIGO-mass black holes as the bulk of dark matter.

Strong-field and multi-messenger lensing

Black holes are usually studied as sources. They are also excellent lenses, and a binary black hole is a lens whose caustic structure moves. When a sub-parsec supermassive binary sits in front of stars in its host galaxy, those caustics sweep across background stars once per orbit, producing a quasi-periodic magnification with a distinctive shape. That turns an existing problem on its head: the systems that will dominate the LISA and pulsar-timing signal are almost impossible to resolve directly, but quasi-periodic lensed starlight offers a way to find them electromagnetically and measure their orbital parameters before their gravitational waves are ever observed. Closer to home, continuous gravitational waves from sources behind Sgr A* can be strongly lensed well beyond the Einstein radius.

Strong fields also change how the wave itself propagates. Near a massive body the standard lensing formalism — weak fields, small deflections, a single flat background — no longer holds: the wave couples to the background curvature at subleading order in the wavelength, and its two polarizations stop following the same path. This gravitational spin-Hall effect has no counterpart in geometric optics.

Multi-messenger lensing is the wider frontier. A lensed gravitational wave and a lensed electromagnetic counterpart of the same event constrain the lens far better than either alone, because they probe complementary regimes: the wave diffracts, the light does not. Building this into a coherent observational strategy across LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA, Einstein Telescope, Rubin and Euclid is a community-scale effort, and I coordinated the microlensing section of the community white paper on it.

Tests of gravity and dark energy

A gravitational wave accumulates any departure from general relativity along its entire path, which makes propagation one of the cleanest tests available. My earlier work identified the speed of gravitational waves as the sharpest discriminant among scalar–tensor theories — a prediction settled spectacularly by GW170817, whose near-simultaneous electromagnetic counterpart ruled out entire classes of dark-energy models in a single stroke.

Lensing sharpens the test, because a lens does what a smooth cosmological background cannot: it breaks the symmetry. In theories beyond general relativity that lets a wave interact with new fields of different spin, couplings that vanish exactly on a homogeneous background. The result is birefringence — the two polarizations travelling at different speeds, the lens acting as an anisotropic crystal — along with dispersion and modified diffraction. This is a test that needs no electromagnetic counterpart, and the bounds we obtained from gravitational-wave data alone already rival those from GW170817.

The programme extends this from the high-frequency expansion to a complete wave-optics formalism, and from specific dark-energy models to a theory-agnostic parameterization of lensing beyond Einstein — the analogue of the post-Newtonian parameters in the Solar System — delivered as a likelihood module for cosmological analyses.

Methods, inference and public software

Wave-optics lensing is computationally awkward: the amplification factor is an oscillatory integral over a Fermat potential with singular structure, and a naive evaluation is far too slow to sit inside a parameter-estimation loop. A large part of the programme is therefore methodological — finding formulations that are both accurate and fast enough to run on catalogues rather than on single events.

The results are public. GLoW implements contour-integration and time-domain methods for general matter distributions and has been used in more than eighteen publications. Alongside it, collaborators lead Glworia for Bayesian parameter estimation and WOlensing for non-symmetric lenses. Everything we publish should be reproducible with code someone else can download.

Speed is not only a convenience. Searching a catalogue means running inference thousands of times, which is where machine learning enters: a network trained on microlensed waveforms can return a posterior in seconds rather than days, making a population-level search feasible at all.

Cosmology and gravity theories

Cosmological tests of dark energy, and the scalar–tensor theories behind them. This is the work that preceded the lensing programme — still active through collaborations and through hi_class — and it is where much of the theoretical machinery the lensing work relies on came from.

Dark energy and cosmic acceleration

Testing the physics behind cosmic acceleration against data, rather than assuming a cosmological constant and stopping there. Recent work re-examined DESI's evidence for dynamical dark energy by reconstructing the dark-energy density directly from the data, without committing to a parameterization — a way of asking what the observations actually require rather than which model fits best.

Earlier work mapped what surveys will be able to say about scalar–tensor theories in general, and showed that the baryon acoustic oscillation scale shifts non-linearly in modified gravity, which matters for how the standard ruler is calibrated.

The Hubble tension

The disagreement between the Hubble constant inferred from the CMB and measured on the distance ladder may be a systematic, or it may be physics. Modified gravity offers a route to the second: the covariant Galileon relieves the tension through several distinct mechanisms within one theory, and gravity in the era of matter–radiation equality can shift H0 without the fine-tuned initial conditions that other solutions require.

  • Gravity in the era of equality: towards solutions to the Hubble problem without fine-tuned initial conditions M. Zumalacárregui — PRD 102, 023523 (2020) Phys. Rev. D 102, 023523 (2020)
  • Galileon gravity in light of ISW, CMB, BAO and H0 data J. Renk, M. Zumalacárregui, F. Montanari, A. Barreira JCAP 10, 020 (2017)

hi_class

An Einstein–Boltzmann solver covering the full Horndeski class, fast and stable enough for Markov-chain analyses. Getting this right is unglamorous and essential: a modified-gravity constraint is only as trustworthy as the code that produced the predictions, so hi_class is validated against independent implementations wherever they exist.

  • hi_class: Horndeski in the Cosmic Linear Anisotropy Solving System M. Zumalacárregui, E. Bellini, I. Sawicki, J. Lesgourgues, P. G. Ferreira — JCAP 08, 019 (2017)

Scalar–tensor gravity beyond Horndeski

Horndeski's theory was long taken to be the most general scalar–tensor theory with second-order equations of motion, and therefore free of the instabilities that plague higher-derivative theories. Generalized field redefinitions show that the boundary sits further out: there are viable theories beyond Horndeski, and I constructed the first explicit examples.

  • Transforming gravity: from derivative couplings to matter to second-order scalar–tensor theories beyond the Horndeski Lagrangian M. Zumalacárregui, J. García-Bellido — PRD 89, 064046 Phys. Rev. D 89, 064046 (2014)
  • Towards the most general scalar–tensor theories of gravity: a unified approach in the language of differential forms J. M. Ezquiaga, J. García-Bellido, M. Zumalacárregui Phys. Rev. D 94, 024005 (2016)
  • Shaken, not stirred: kinetic mixing in scalar–tensor theories of gravity D. Bettoni, M. Zumalacárregui Phys. Rev. D 91, 104009 (2015)

Disformal transformations

A field redefinition that mixes the metric with derivatives of a scalar field generalizes the familiar Einstein and Jordan frames. The consequences are concrete: disformally coupled theories turn out to be DBI Galileons in disguise, and the same coupling screens fifth forces in dense environments, hiding the scalar where laboratory tests would otherwise have found it.

  • Screening modifications of gravity through disformally coupled fields T. Koivisto, D. Mota, M. Zumalacárregui — Phys. Rev. Lett. 109, 241102
  • DBI Galileons in the Einstein frame: local gravity and cosmology M. Zumalacárregui, T. Koivisto, D. Mota — PRD 87, 083010 Phys. Rev. D 87, 083010 (2013)
  • Disformal scalar fields and the dark sector of the Universe M. Zumalacárregui, T. Koivisto, D. Mota, P. Ruiz-Lapuente JCAP 05, 038 (2010)

Inhomogeneous cosmologies

Before dark energy was assumed, one alternative was that we sit near the centre of a very large underdensity, with apparent acceleration produced by inhomogeneity rather than by a new component. Testing that idea properly turned out to be useful well beyond its own merits: it exposes which steps in a standard analysis quietly assume homogeneity, and cosmic rulers strain the void models badly once supernovae, BAO and the CMB are combined.

Scientific coordination and future observatories

Present detectors are the beginning of gravitational-wave astronomy. Much of my community work goes into shaping what comes next, and into making sure lensing has a place in it.

LISA

LISA is where wave-optics lensing stops being a curiosity. At millihertz frequencies the wavelengths are long enough that diffraction by dark-matter halos becomes detectable — and, left unmodelled, could contaminate the whole data stream. Preparing the mission for that is part of the Cosmology Working Group's remit.

Consortium member since 2018; Council member and Senior Co-Chair of the Cosmology Working Group since 2025. Section coordinator for gravitational-wave lensing in the LISA Cosmology white paper.

  • Cosmology with the Laser Interferometer Space Antenna P. Auclair et al. [LISA Cosmology Working Group]

Euclid

Euclid maps both the galaxies that host gravitational-wave sources and the structures that lens them, which is what makes the connection worth building: the same survey supplies the redshifts for standard sirens and the lens catalogues for multi-messenger lensing.

Consortium member since 2014; lead of the Science Working Group on Gravitational Waves and of its "bright sirens" work package since 2025. Member of the Strong Lensing, Cosmology Theory, and Supernovae & Transients working groups.

GW-Space 2050

The scientific case for ESA's gravitational-wave missions after LISA. Decisions taken now about frequency band and sensitivity determine what is measurable decades from now, so the cosmography case has to be made before the instrument is designed rather than after. The μHz band is a particular opportunity: it reaches sources no other detector will see, and lensed events become more common the further out a detector can reach.

Coordinator for the cosmography section since 2023. The report is expected shortly.

  • Unveiling the gravitational Universe at μHz frequencies A. Sesana et al.

Future detectors and mission concepts

Next-generation ground detectors will see far enough that lensed events stop being rare, which changes what a lensing search has to be able to do. GUEST is a separate concept altogether, detecting gravitational waves by tracking satellites.
